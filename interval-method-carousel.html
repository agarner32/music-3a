Interval Method — Worked Examples

Six examples walking through the major scale method step by step.
The last example shows what to do when the bottom note is not a major scale root.

The Four Rules
Reference

Use these rules any time you need to adjust an interval quality — whether you are identifying an interval or constructing one. They work in any direction: raising or lowering either note, as long as the size stays the same.

1
If you make a major interval smaller by a half step, it becomes minor.
2
If you make a major interval smaller by two half steps, it becomes diminished.
3
If you make a perfect interval smaller by a half step, it becomes diminished.
4
If you make either a major or perfect interval larger by a half step, it becomes augmented.

E♭ to G
Major
1
Count the size: E♭(1) – F(2) – G(3) → a third.
2
Treat E♭ as the tonic. E♭ major has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. The third scale degree of E♭ major is G♮.
3
G♮ is in E♭ major. Thirds are in the major/minor group. If the top note is in the major scale, the interval is major.
Answer
Major 3rd M3
D to F
Minor
1
Count the size: D(1) – E(2) – F(3) → a third.
2
Treat D as the tonic. D major has two sharps: F♯ and C♯. The third scale degree of D major is F♯, which means D to F♯ would be a major 3rd.
3
The top note is F♮, not F♯. F♮ makes the interval a half step smaller than a major 3rd. By Rule 1, a major interval made smaller by a half step becomes minor.
Answer
Minor 3rd m3
G to D
Perfect
1
Count the size: G(1) – A(2) – B(3) – C(4) – D(5) → a fifth.
2
Treat G as the tonic. G major has one sharp: F♯. The fifth scale degree of G major is D♮.
3
D♮ is in G major. Fifths are in the perfect group. If the top note is in the major scale, the interval is perfect.
Answer
Perfect 5th P5
F to B
Augmented
1
Count the size: F(1) – G(2) – A(3) – B(4) → a fourth.
2
Treat F as the tonic. F major has one flat: B♭. The fourth scale degree of F major is B♭, which means F to B♭ would be a perfect 4th.
3
The top note is B♮, not B♭. B♮ makes the interval a half step larger than a perfect 4th. By Rule 4, a perfect interval made larger by a half step becomes augmented. This interval is also called the tritone.
Answer
Augmented 4th A4
E to C
Minor
1
Count the size: E(1) – F(2) – G(3) – A(4) – B(5) – C(6) → a sixth.
2
Treat E as the tonic. E major has four sharps: F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯. The sixth scale degree of E major is C♯, which means E to C♯ would be a major 6th.
3
The top note is C♮, not C♯. C♮ makes the interval a half step smaller than a major 6th. By Rule 1, a major interval made smaller by a half step becomes minor.
Answer
Minor 6th m6
D♯ to B
Difficult Bottom Note
1
Count the size: D(1) – E(2) – F(3) – G(4) – A(5) – B(6) → a sixth. (Accidentals never affect size.)
2
Remove the accidental from the bottom note. Temporarily treat the bottom note as D♮ instead of D♯. D to B: D major has F♯ and C♯ — B♮ is in D major. D to B = major 6th.
3
Put the accidental back. Restoring D♯ raises the bottom note by a half step. Raising the bottom note makes the interval smaller by a half step.
4
Apply The Four Rules: making a major interval smaller by a half step = minor. D♯ to B is a minor 6th.
Answer
Minor 6th m6
